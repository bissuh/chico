# story-craft

A story is a funnel. Attention to trust to belief to action. This skill is how Chico produces stories that move readers down that funnel and closes the loop with a CTA that feels inevitable, not bolted on.

Source material: the six-part storytelling-for-growth framework distilled from the founder transcript on stories, the Zeigarnik effect, and the content waterfall. Reduced to operating instructions below.

## When to invoke

- Drafting any narrative-driven content under the TBP brand: newsletter openers and arcs, X threads, LinkedIn posts, lead-magnet hooks, video scripts, sales-page sections.
- Rewriting a Bissuh draft (with his sign-off — Yellow tier) when the structure is mushy or the CTA jolts the reader out of the story.
- Reviewing a story to diagnose which of the five lines is killing conversion.
- Mining a customer interview, a session note, or a raw memory for a story arc that earns attention.
- Whenever the question is "why isn't this landing?" and the artifact is a story.

## When NOT to invoke

- Pure data reports, weekly reviews, or operational summaries. Those want clarity, not narrative arc. Use the right skill for that work.
- Anything that goes out under the Saturday newsletter byline if Bissuh hasn't asked for help. Saturday is Bissuh's anchor. Don't apply this skill there unsolicited.
- Internal documentation, specs, or memory files. Stories are a public-facing tool.

## Three modes

Pick one before you start. State it at the top of your output.

### Mode 1: CREATE

Start from raw material and build a story from scratch. Inputs:
- A topic or angle ("why most builders quit at month 3")
- A real moment (a customer call, a Bissuh memory, a deli-owner-style anchor)
- A target outcome (subscribe, reply, click a lead magnet, book a call)

Output: a complete story mapped to the 5-line framework, with hook variants and a coupled CTA.

### Mode 2: REWRITE

Take an existing draft and tighten it through the framework. Inputs:
- The draft text
- What it's trying to do (the target outcome)

Output: the rewritten story, plus a short diff note saying which lines were weak and what changed.

### Mode 3: REVIEW

Diagnose where a story breaks. No rewriting unless asked. Inputs:
- The draft text
- Optional: performance signal ("CTR is 0.4%, well below 3% target")

Output: a 5-line scorecard — which line is strong, which is weak, which is missing — and the single change that would unblock it. Optionally followed by a one-paragraph proposed fix for the weakest line.

## The 5-line framework

This is the backbone. Every story Chico writes maps to these five lines. Skip one and the story breaks.

1. **Mirror — where the reader is right now.**
   The reader thinks "this guy is reading my mind." Specific, lived, sensory. Not "many founders feel stuck." Yes "you close the laptop at 11pm, refresh the dashboard one more time, and tell yourself the numbers will be different in the morning."
   - Test: would the reader's spouse recognize this scene?

2. **Friction — why they're stuck, including the personal cost.**
   Not just the business problem. The relationship damage, the missed birthdays, the 3am wake-ups, the partner asking when it slows down. Business problems are abstract. Personal costs are addictive.
   - Test: does the friction explain why the obvious fix hasn't worked yet?

3. **Realization — the epiphany no one tells them.**
   One line. Counter-intuitive. The thing the reader hasn't been told because everyone else is selling them the wrong fix. "The problem isn't your work ethic. You ARE the system."
   - Test: if the reader nods at this line, do they also feel slightly stupid for not seeing it themselves?

4. **Shift — the transformation.**
   Concrete before/after. Numbers when you have them. Texture when you don't. "15-hour days to 4-hour days. Burnt out to energized. Trapped to free." Not vague claims like "everything changed."
   - Test: can the reader picture themselves in the after state without squinting?

5. **Invitation — the inevitable next step.**
   The CTA grows out of the loop the story opened. If the story was about removing yourself from operations, the CTA is the Operator-Replacement Checklist, not a generic "subscribe to the newsletter." A good CTA closes the loop the story opened.
   - Test: if you cut the CTA and gave it to a stranger, would they correctly guess which story it belonged to?

## Make the story addictive (the inside of the lines)

The 5 lines are the skeleton. These tactics are the muscle. Apply them inside the lines.

**Open a loop in the first 2 sentences and don't close it for a while.**
Loops are unfinished business. The brain hates unfinished business. "Two government agents walked into my office and told me I had 30 days before they shut us down." Then keep going. Don't tell them what happened until the reader has earned it. Aim for at least one open loop per story, ideally two or three stacked.

**Stack curiosity gaps.**
Every section should leave a small unanswered question that the next section pays off. A story without curiosity gaps in the middle gets a great open and a 12-second average read time.

**Use the 5-year test for source material.**
What did Bissuh wish someone had shown him 5 years ago? Those moments. The raid on BitMaker. The decision to move countries. The first hire that didn't work. Polish kills authenticity. Specific chaos earns trust.

**The thumbnail-vs-retention split.**
Intrigue drives the click. Tension keeps them in. The headline / opening line is intrigue. The body is tension. Don't confuse them. An intriguing headline with a tension-free body is a bait-and-switch. A tension-filled body with a flat headline never gets read.

## Couple the CTA to the loop

This is where most drafts die. The story is great, then the writer staples on "subscribe to my newsletter" and the reader's nervous system reboots.

Rules:
- The CTA names the smallest, most valuable thing that starts to solve the problem the story surfaced.
- The CTA reuses the story's language. If the story said "you ARE the system," the CTA references the system, not "growth tips."
- The CTA is generous. Free, useful, immediate. The bar: if this is free, what's in the paid stuff?
- The CTA does not break the spell. No "anyway," "in any case," or "if this resonates." Just the next step.

Example couple:
- Story line: "You ARE the system. The reason you can't take a vacation is that every workflow has your fingerprints on it."
- Coupled CTA: "Here's the Operator-Replacement Checklist I used to pull my fingerprints off 17 workflows in 60 days. Free. Reply 'system' and I'll send it."

## The content waterfall

After a story works, multiply it. Don't invent a new story for every channel.

One core story → newsletter long-form essay → X thread (5-7 posts, each post is one beat from the 5 lines) → LinkedIn post (mirror + friction + realization, friction-heavy) → IG carousel (5 slides, one per line) → short-form video (mirror + invitation only, friction implied).

Pick winners to waterfall. A story that opened well but didn't convert isn't a winner. A story that converted is. Reuse the converters until they stop converting. Then retire them.

## Procedure

When invoked, work through these steps in order. Show your work in the output so Bissuh can audit the thinking.

1. **State the mode.** CREATE, REWRITE, or REVIEW. State the target outcome (subscribe, reply, click, book a call).

2. **Resolve source material.**
   - CREATE: confirm the topic/moment/angle. If it's thin, ask Bissuh one question to deepen it before drafting.
   - REWRITE: read the existing draft once for shape, once for structure.
   - REVIEW: read the draft twice. Don't rewrite yet.

3. **Map to the 5 lines.** Always. Even in REVIEW mode, write the map. The map shows what's missing more than the prose does.

4. **Draft or score.**
   - CREATE / REWRITE: draft the story with the 5 lines clearly hit. Then read it once and check for AI tells, em dashes, vague claims. Then check that the CTA is coupled, not stapled.
   - REVIEW: score each line on a 1-3 scale (1 = missing or broken, 2 = present but weak, 3 = strong). Identify the single weakest line. Suggest one specific change.

5. **Quality gate before delivery.**
   - No AI tells: "furthermore," "moreover," "in conclusion," "it's worth noting," "delve into," "leverage," "unleash," "elevate," "this isn't just X, it's Y."
   - No em dashes. Use periods, commas, or rewrite.
   - Short paragraphs, street-level language.
   - Specific numbers where they exist. "$400M annual revenue." Not "massive company."
   - Show the reader themselves. Not "many founders." Yes "you, at 11pm, refreshing Stripe."

6. **Hand off.**
   - CREATE / REWRITE: drop the artifact in `outbox/` with status `review-needed` and a one-line note about which line did the heavy lifting.
   - REVIEW: write the scorecard back to the invoker. If Bissuh asks for the fix, switch to REWRITE mode for the weak line only.

```markdown
**Target outcome:** [subscribe / reply / click / book]
**Best line:** [which of the 5 carries this one]

## The story

[The full prose, with the 5 lines blended into a single narrative. Do not label the lines in the prose — labels break the spell. Label them in the map below instead.]

## The 5-line map

1. Mirror: [the sentence(s) that do this]
2. Friction: [...]
3. Realization: [...]
4. Shift: [...]
5. Invitation: [the coupled CTA]
```

```markdown

**Target outcome:** [...]
**Verdict:** [where this story breaks in one sentence]

## Scorecard
```

## Examples to study, not to copy

- The BitMaker government-raid story: a textbook 5-line arc. Mirror (newly proud founder, 7 months in, $200k MRR). Friction (two agents, jail threat, $1M fine, business cratering). Realization (credentials don't save you, only mass authenticity does). Shift (3,000 emails in 48 hours, government backed off, trained 2,000 engineers, acquired). Invitation (implicit: tell your story before you need it).
- The Skims-vs-Shopify-shapewear comparison: same product, same factories, different story. Use this when explaining why authenticity beats specs.
- Felix vs TBP: the divergence story. Felix removed the human. TBP keeps the human in. The story IS the differentiation.

## Calibration: what good looks like

A finished story that earns its keep:
- Opens a loop in the first two sentences.
- Names the reader's exact 11pm scene by line 3.
- Has at least one specific number or named entity (a person, a company, a dollar figure, a date).
- Has at least one "I was wrong about X" moment from the narrator.
- Closes with a CTA that reuses the story's language and offers one concrete next step.
- Reads in under 3 minutes for newsletter, under 30 seconds for X, under 10 seconds for video hook.
