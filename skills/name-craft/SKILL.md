# name-craft

A name is the highest-frequency, longest-duration thing a brand owns. The right name compounds; the wrong one creates friction every single day. This skill is how Chico produces names that earn attention, hold it, and surprise — using the method behind Pentium, Blackberry, Swiffer, Sonos, Azure, Impossible, Febreze, Slimfast, and Windsurf.

Source material: David Placek's process at Lexicon Branding, distilled to operating instructions below. Background reading is in `knowledge-base/naming/` if it exists; if not, the rules in this file are the source of truth.

## When to invoke

- Naming a new company, product, feature, lead magnet, course, internal tool, recurring section, or content series.
- A rename is on the table (Codeium → Windsurf situation: SEO bad, can't spell it, no one remembers it).
- An existing candidate name feels flat and the asker can't say why.
- Reviewing a shortlist someone else generated to find the polarizing-but-right pick hiding in the trash pile.
- Whenever someone asks "is X a good name?" — run EVALUATE mode, don't freelance an opinion.

## When NOT to invoke

- Naming a file, variable, function, or git branch. That's craft, not branding.
- Saturday-newsletter section names if Bissuh hasn't asked. Saturday is his anchor.
- Naming something that already has stickiness in the marketplace where the cost of change exceeds the benefit. Run a rename audit first (see RENAME mode).
- Anything where the asker just wants validation. Names are decisions, and this skill argues the position. If the asker wants a yes-machine, redirect them.

## Three modes

Pick one before you start. State it at the top of the output.

### Mode 1: CREATE

Start from a blank page and produce a shortlist. Inputs:
- What is the thing (product, company, feature, etc.) and what does it do
- Who is it for
- Where does it live (category, competitive set, channel)
- Domain need: yes / no / "would be nice"

Output: framing answers, three divergent baskets, ~10-15 finalists with scores and domain status, top 3 with proof-of-concept mockups, one polarizing pick.

### Mode 2: EVALUATE

Score one candidate or a small shortlist someone else generated. Inputs:
- The candidate(s)
- The thing being named (same brief as CREATE)

Output: a 3-criterion scorecard per name (original / processing fluent / unexpected) plus sound-symbolism notes, plus a verdict and the single change that would unblock the weakest candidate.

### Mode 3: RENAME

Argue whether to rename, and if yes, produce the replacement. Inputs:
- The current name
- Why it might be wrong (SEO, search, pronunciation, legal, polarization in the wrong direction, market drift)
- What's stuck to the current name (URL, trademark, audience, press)

Output: a kill-or-keep recommendation with the case, then — if kill — a CREATE pass biased toward the rename use case (story for the switch, equity transfer plan, launch energy).

## The Placek method (the engine)

This is the backbone. Skip a step and the names go flat.

### 1. The four framing questions

Answer these before generating a single name. Write them at the top of the output. Most bad names come from skipping this step and going straight to brainstorming.

1. **How do you define winning here?** Not "we want a good name." What does the name need to do — own a category, signal a category shift, slip past a comfortable incumbent, command a price premium?
2. **What do you have to win?** What's true about the product, team, or moment that no competitor can claim?
3. **What do you need to win?** What's the gap — attention, credibility, courage, distance from a stale category?
4. **What do you need to say?** What's the one thing the name has to communicate (often a feeling, not a feature) the second a stranger hears it?

If the asker can't answer these, ask one sharp question to surface them. Don't generate until they're locked.

### 2. The three baskets

Generate names in three divergent passes. Quantity → quality. Target 60-100 raw names per basket, not 10-15 total.

- **Basket A — inside the brief.** Names that know everything about the product. Closest to the obvious answer, but pushed past comfortable. This basket is most likely to produce names trademark will clear.
- **Basket B — adjacent angle.** Add an unexpected ingredient to the brief and re-generate. (For a fiber product, add "energy" or "lightness." For a CRM, add "memory" or "field notes.") This basket finds the breakthrough.
- **Basket C — different category entirely.** Generate as if naming something else (athletic performance, aviation, music, weather) using only the *feeling* of the brief. This basket finds the asymmetric win — the Impossible, the Blackberry, the Swiffer.

Most naming projects die because they only run Basket A.

### 3. Treasure-hunt databases

Don't sit and stare. Pull from these and let synchronicity do the work. Aim to spend 15-30 minutes per pull, then move on.

- **Real-word familiar-yet-surprising.** Common English words that are unexpected in context. (Apple in computers, Blackberry in mobile, Impossible in food.)
- **Latin and Greek roots.** Especially for tech, science, premium positioning.
- **Mythology — Greek, Roman, Norse, regional.** For premium, mystery, or archetype-anchored brands.
- **Periodic table and chemistry.** For tech with a "fundamental element" feel (Pentium-style).
- **Aviation, aerodynamics, weather.** For lightness, speed, dynamics. Spend 15 minutes on "what makes a wing lift" and harvest the vocabulary.
- **Music, instruments, performance.** For sensory or experiential brands (Sonos lives here).
- **Geography, cartography, exploration.** For brands about discovery, frontier, distance.
- **Adjacent-domain pulls.** Pick a category nothing like yours and harvest its vocabulary. (For a fiber product: read a magazine you've never read. For a CRM: read a book on watchmaking.)
- **Coined / morphological.** Build new words from morphemes (Pentium = penta + -ium). Use sparingly — coined names carry a high "teach the market" cost unless the structure is processing-fluent.

For TBP work specifically: also pull from Brazilian Portuguese, soccer culture, regional Brazilian references, and `memory/bissuh.md` for source material that ties names to the founder story.

### 4. Sound symbolism cheat sheet

Apply when scoring finalists. The brain processes sounds before meaning.

- **K, P, B, D** — plosives. Speed, strength, decisiveness. (Kodak, Pepsi, Blackberry, Dropbox.)
- **X** — innovation, technology, edge. (Lexus, SpaceX, Xerox, Pentium-adjacent.)
- **Z** — fast, modern, slightly disruptive. (Zoom, Zara, Azure.)
- **S, F, soft consonants** — calm, premium, sensory. (Sonos, Swiffer for "soft sweep," Febreze for "fresh breeze.")
- **L, M, N, soft vowels** — comfort, warmth, intimacy. (Mama, Nest.)
- **CVCV pattern (consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel)** — first sound shape humans learn. Memorable across languages. (Sonos, Lexus, Toto, Coco.)

Hard names start with hard consonants. Soft names start with soft consonants. Mismatches between sound and positioning are why names feel "off."

### 5. The comfort trap

The single most important slide in Placek's deck. Most teams settle in the wrong zone.

```
BIZARRE / ABSURD ←——— APPROXIMATE THINKING ———→ SAFE / WORKABLE
       ↑                       ↑                         ↑
    Tension zone        Where good names live     Invisible zone
   (polarizing,         (familiar yet            (consensus,
    high energy)         surprising)              forgotten)
```

- **Polarization is signal, not noise.** If half the team hates it, that's energy. Pentium, Blackberry, Impossible were all rejected first. Andy Grove on Pentium: "It's polarizing — that means it has energy."
- **Comfort is death.** Pro-Mop tested better than Swiffer in research. Pro-Mop became Ready Mop and did $200M. Swiffer did $5B.
- **Approximate thinking is the operating zone.** Push generation toward bizarre. Walk finalists back to approximate. Never settle in safe.

### 6. The three filters

Every finalist must pass all three. Score 1-3 per filter (1 = fails, 2 = present but weak, 3 = strong). A name with two 3s and a 1 is not a finalist.

1. **Original.** In *this* category, in *this* moment. Not original in absolute terms — original in context. Azure was original at Microsoft because their portfolio was descriptive. Azure at Google would have been expected.
2. **Processing fluent.** Pronounceable on first read. Spellable on first hear. Familiar enough that the brain doesn't reject it, surprising enough that the brain leans in.
3. **Unexpected.** Surprises in context. The reader's eyebrow goes up. Not weird for weird's sake — unexpected because it's right.

If a candidate scores 3-3-1 (fails on unexpected), it's a Ready Mop. Send it back.

## Domain validation (dotchk integration)

`dotchk` is installed at `/Users/bissuh/.cargo/bin/dotchk`. It's a fast NS-record-based domain checker. Always WHOIS-verify before purchase (purchase is Red tier — see hard rules).

### When to ask about domains

At the start of every CREATE or RENAME run, ask:

> Does this name need a domain? If yes, which TLDs matter — `.com` is table stakes, `.ai` for AI products, `.io` for dev tools, `.co` as a fallback. Anything else?

If the answer is no (it's a feature name, an internal tool, a section title), skip the rest of this section.

### Standard playbook

For each top-3 finalist, run:

```bash
dotchk tld <name> --popular
```

This sweeps the popular TLDs and reports what's available. Report results inline with each finalist.

### Pattern search (when shortlist is thin)

When the brief calls for short brandable .com / .ai / .io domains and the shortlist isn't producing winners:

```bash
# 4-7 letter brandable .com
dotchk pattern "[a-z]{4,7}\.com" --limit 100

# Themed pattern: prefix + word
dotchk pattern "(get|use|try)[a-z]{4,8}\.com"

# .ai with a domain seed
dotchk pattern "[a-z]{4,8}(ai|ml)\.ai"
```

Use the output as raw material to feed back into Basket B or C. Available short domains often *suggest* names — let synchronicity work both directions.

### Bulk check

When evaluating a shortlist of 10-20 finalists across one TLD:

```bash
echo -e "name1.com\nname2.com\nname3.com" | dotchk bulk /dev/stdin --stats
```

Or write the list to a temp file and pass it in.

### Reporting domain status

In the output, mark each finalist with one of:

- `✓ <tld>` — available per dotchk (NS-record check, not WHOIS-verified)
- `✗ <tld>` — taken
- `~ <tld>` — NS-record says available but the name is a real word likely to be premium-priced or held by a registrar; flag for WHOIS check before celebrating
- `· <tld>` — not checked (out of scope for this brief)

Hard rule: never recommend "buy this domain" as part of the deliverable. Domain purchase is Red tier. Surface availability, the asker decides.

## Procedure

When invoked, work through these steps in order. Show your work in the output so Bissuh can audit the thinking.

1. **State the mode.** CREATE / EVALUATE / RENAME.

2. **Lock the brief.** Write the four framing questions and answer them. If any answer is thin, ask one sharp question before generating.

3. **Domain question.** Ask about TLDs if a public-facing name. Skip if internal.

4. **Generate (CREATE) or score (EVALUATE) or audit (RENAME).**
   - CREATE: run all three baskets. Aim for 60-100 raw names per basket. Don't self-edit during generation. Then cull to 10-15 finalists.
   - EVALUATE: score each candidate against the three filters and sound symbolism. Identify the weakest filter for each.
   - RENAME: write the kill-or-keep case first. If kill, run CREATE.

5. **Score the finalists.** Three filters (original / processing fluent / unexpected) on 1-3 scale. Sound symbolism note. Domain status if applicable.

6. **Identify the polarizing pick.** Among the finalists, which one will half the room hate? That's often the right answer. Call it out explicitly even if it's not the safest top-3.

7. **Proof-of-concept the top 3.** For each, write one of:
   - A WSJ-style headline ("Why [Name] just raised $40M to rebuild [category]")
   - A shelf or product context ("Walking down the aisle, you see [competitor], [competitor], and then [Name].")
   - A reader-encounter moment ("First time you hear it, you ask the friend who said it to repeat it.")
   The test: is it believable in less than a second?

8. **Quality gate before delivery.**
   - No AI tells, no em dashes (inherits from `CLAUDE.md` and `TBP-WRITING-MANUAL.md`).
   - Every finalist has a score, a sound note, and a domain status (if relevant).
   - The polarizing pick is named explicitly.
   - The losers list is preserved (see hard rules).

9. **Hand off.**
   - CREATE / RENAME: drop the deliverable in `outbox/` with status `review-needed` if the name will go public (Yellow tier). Otherwise return inline.
   - EVALUATE: return the scorecard inline.

## Calibration: what good looks like

A finished CREATE deliverable that earns its keep:

- Four framing answers are specific, not generic.
- Three baskets are visibly different from each other. If Basket C looks like Basket A, the generation didn't diverge enough.
- At least 10 finalists, ideally 15. Each scored on three filters plus sound.
- Top 3 each have a proof-of-concept that survives the "is this believable in under a second" test.
- The polarizing pick is named and the case for it is made even when it isn't the safest top-3.
- Domain status is reported for finalists when a domain matters.
- A losers list is preserved.

A finished EVALUATE that earns its keep:

- Scorecard is honest, including 1s. (If everything scores 3, you didn't evaluate, you flattered.)
- The single weakest filter is identified per name.
- The fix is specific to the filter that fails, not a generic "make it stronger."
