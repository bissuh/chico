# conversion-craft

Traffic is a multiplier on a rate you already have. Ten thousand visitors onto a page that converts at zero percent is still zero customers, and you learned nothing except that the page is broken. conversion-craft fixes the rate: the offer and the page and the funnel, so that when the traffic arrives it turns into sales instead of bouncing.

This is the skill that runs *after* positioning and *before* the distribution skills. positioning answers what you sell and to whom. The channel skills bring eyeballs. conversion-craft is the part in the middle that decides whether those eyeballs become customers. Skip it and every dollar of traffic amplifies a leak.

**Before you start:** read the project's `brand.md`, especially the offer, the primary conversion goal, the price, and the audience. conversion-craft assumes positioning is already sharp. If the offer is still a vitamin or the audience is "everyone," stop and run `turma:positioning` first. No page converts a vitamin.

## When to invoke

- Before spending on any acquisition, paid or organic. Conversion first, traffic second. Always.
- When the top of the funnel is fine but the bottom isn't: "we get visitors, they don't buy."
- When designing or pricing an offer, or when the current pricing model feels like it's fighting the sale.
- When a landing page needs building, or an existing one is leaking and you can't say where.
- Right after positioning is settled, as the gate every distribution skill has to clear before it earns its budget.

## When NOT to invoke

- When positioning isn't settled. A vague offer or a "for everyone" audience is a positioning problem. Go to `turma:positioning`; conversion work on top of fuzzy positioning just polishes the leak.
- For line-level copy voice. That's `turma:story-craft`, `turma:optimize-post`, `turma:anti-ai-linguo`. conversion-craft calls them for the copy layer; it doesn't replace them.
- For the acquisition channels themselves. Where the traffic comes from is `turma:power-law`, `turma:seo-strategy`, `turma:social-posts`, `turma:cta-machine`, `turma:ghostshelf`.
- For what happens after the yes. Whether the customer stays is `turma:retention-craft`. This skill stops at the conversion; that one starts there.
- For a mobile app's store listing specifically. The product page is a landing page and every rule here applies, but the fields, the A/B mechanics, and the ranking consequences are `turma:aso-strategy`.
- For a standalone lead magnet. That's `turma:micromagnet-craft`. The two share the "give quick value free, then sell" logic, but micromagnet builds the top-of-funnel handraiser while conversion-craft makes the core offer and page convert.

## The rule that governs everything: conversion before traffic

Fix the rate before you buy the traffic. The order is not a preference, it's arithmetic. Traffic multiplies whatever conversion rate the page already has, so a thousand dollars of ads onto a page that doesn't convert buys nothing and teaches nothing. Worse, it burns the one budget an underdog can't refill.

It's also the cheapest order. Conversion work costs your time. Traffic costs money, and money only compounds a rate that already works. Rewriting the offer, collecting real testimonials, recording a product demo: all of it is unglamorous and free. Chasing traffic is more fun, which is exactly why projects do it first and lose.

This reconciles the paid-channel question directly. Paid acquisition is for *accelerating a funnel that already converts*, never for *finding out* whether it converts. Prove the rate on cheap or organic traffic, then pour paid on top. `turma:power-law` owns which channels to buy; this skill owns whether you've earned the right to buy any yet.

## Layer 1: the offer

Two moves decide whether the offer sells before a single word of copy is written.

**Monetization follows retention.** The pricing model has to match how often the product actually delivers value. Recurring value earns a recurring price. One-time or occasional value earns a one-time price.

- A tool used daily or weekly (a social scheduler, a support inbox) monetizes cleanly as a monthly or annual subscription. The value shows up on the same cadence as the charge.
- A tool used twice a year (a travel itinerary builder, a tax-season helper) does not. Ask a customer to pay monthly for something they touch in March and forget, and they do the math and cancel. That product monetizes as a one-time pass, a credit pack, or a lifetime deal.

The customer does not care that you want passive income. They care about paying a fair price to solve their problem, and most of the time the fair price is "pay once and stop thinking about it." Charging a subscription against non-recurring value is one of the most common silent conversion killers, because the leak is at the pricing model, not the page. Diagnose it by asking one question: how often does a real user get value from this? Let the honest answer pick the model.

**Make it a no-brainer.** Most of the revenue lives in the gap between "I'll buy it right now" and "I'll think about it." A no-brainer offer feels, to the buyer, almost like a rip-off in their favor. That is not the same as cutting the price until the project is unprofitable. It means removing friction from the yes:

- Make crystal clear what they get.
- Make it obvious the thing is worth more than the ask.
- Make it easy to actually pay.

You can raise the price for later customers and introduce soft usage limits once you have momentum. Early on, momentum beats margin, because a customer who bought is a customer who can refer, testify, and renew. A price no one says yes to has a margin of zero.

## Layer 2: the page

The landing page is roughly eighty percent of the marketing. If the page can't convert, nailing every other thing barely matters, because all of it eventually points here. If the page does convert, traffic compounds into a sales machine. So the page gets fixed before the traffic gets chased.

**Show the product, don't describe it.** People want to see the thing and use the thing. Reading about it is boring; watching it do the job is not. A demo in the hero block, feature GIFs, a free trial or an interactive demo will out-convert the wittiest headline every time. No visuals, no desire. Let the reader watch the product remove the pain before you ask them to pay for it.

**The high-converting page has six parts.** Audit an existing page against all six; build a new one from all six. Miss one and the page leaks there.

1. A deep read of the target audience (comes from `turma:positioning` and `brand.md`, not invented here).
2. One clear value proposition. The single sentence a stranger nods at.
3. Desirable product visuals. Demo, GIF, screenshots, the product in motion.
4. Believable social proof. Specific and named ("cut our close from 6 days to 1"), not "join 10,000 happy users."
5. Copy that's enjoyable to read. This is where `turma:story-craft` and `turma:anti-ai-linguo` run their pass.
6. A CTA that feels like the obvious next step, not a leap off a cliff.

**Write like a human.** Not an emotionless engineer, not an overcaffeinated sales rep. A normal person saying it out loud. Short sentences. Bullet lists over endless paragraphs, and product visuals over bullet lists. Specific and down-to-earth beats grand and Nike-shaped, because a small brand earns trust by sounding like a person, not a billboard. The test is blunt: read the page to a friend and watch their face. Iterate until they get it without you explaining. `turma:anti-ai-linguo` is the final gate here, because AI-flavored copy is the fastest way to sound like every other page the reader has already ignored.

**Test the visuals, do not trust your taste.** Your confidence about a page you designed is worth nothing, and it is expensive when it is wrong. Sebastian Röhl paid a designer to rebuild HabitKit's App Store screenshots, was certain the polished set would win, ran the A/B test, and the original self-made screenshots converted better. One result does not prove that amateur beats professional. It proves that the taste of the person closest to the product is not evidence. Test the hero visual and the headline wherever the platform gives you a native test (Apple's Product Page Optimization, Google Play's store listing experiments, or a split test on your own site), and read nothing into an underpowered one: a page with a few dozen daily visitors cannot reach significance, so decide there with judgment and revisit when the traffic exists.

## Layer 3: the funnel across awareness

Every market holds three kinds of people:

1. Don't have the problem, or don't yet know they have it.
2. Have the problem, but aren't looking for a solution right now.
3. Actively looking for a solution right now.

It is tempting to build only for group three. They're closest to buying. But they're also the smallest slice, and a page built only for them wastes everyone else who arrives one step earlier in the journey.

- **Hot (actively looking):** they'll buy if you don't get in the way. A clear offer, a clear CTA, and then get out of the way.
- **Warm and cold (have the problem but aren't shopping, or don't know yet):** they need warming, not harvesting. Testimonials, FAQs, a free trial, a reactivation or nurture sequence, useful content that keeps the project top of mind for the day the pain sharpens. These are not tire-kickers. They are future sales you haven't earned yet.

A funnel that only harvests the hot leaves most of the market on the table. Build the warm-up path alongside the buy-now path. This is where `turma:micromagnet-craft` plugs in a handraiser to catch the not-yet-ready, and where `turma:story-craft` writes the nurture content that earns the eventual yes.

## Modes

State the mode at the top of your output.

### Mode 1: AUDIT

Given an existing offer and page (and any reachable analytics), score all three layers and find the leak. Output: where the offer's pricing model fits or fights its retention, whether the offer reads as a no-brainer, the page scored against the six parts (which are present, which are missing or weak), whether the funnel serves all three awareness stages or only the hot, and then the single change with the most upside, named concretely with the skill that ships it. If the real problem is upstream (the offer is a vitamin, the audience is vague), say so and hand back to `turma:positioning`.

### Mode 2: BUILD

Given sharp positioning, build the conversion engine from scratch. Output: the pricing model that matches the retention pattern (with the reasoning), the no-brainer framing of the offer, the six-part page (each part filled or handed to the skill that fills it), and the awareness-stage funnel plan (the buy-now path and the warm-up path). Leave price as a number for the owner to set; surface the model and the range, not the final tag.

## How it composes with other skills

- Runs **after** `turma:positioning`. That skill establishes the painkiller and the segment; this one assumes both are sharp. If they aren't, conversion-craft stops and hands back.
- Runs **before** the distribution skills (`turma:power-law`, `turma:seo-strategy`, `turma:social-posts`, `turma:cta-machine`, `turma:ghostshelf`). They bring traffic. This skill is what makes traffic worth bringing, and the gate each of them clears before earning a budget.
- Hands off to `turma:retention-craft` at the yes. The monetization-follows-retention question in Layer 1 is the same cadence question that skill opens with, so a disagreement between the two means one of them is wrong about the product.
- Calls the copy skills for Layer 2: `turma:story-craft` for the narrative, `turma:optimize-post` to score and tighten, `turma:anti-ai-linguo` as the final voice pass.
- Shares the "quick free value, then sell" logic with `turma:micromagnet-craft`. micromagnet builds the top-of-funnel handraiser for the not-yet-ready; conversion-craft makes the core offer and page convert the ready.
- Reads and updates `brand.md`: the offer, the primary conversion goal, the price, the scoreboard. When a conversion fix changes any of those, log it (factual updates are Green; a material offer repositioning is Yellow, same line as `turma:positioning`).

## Source

Distilled from Dan Kulkov's "20 marketing changes to get unstuck" (FounderPal, MakerBox), specifically its Funnel section: monetization-follows-retention, the no-brainer offer, show-the-product, the landing-page checklist, write-like-a-human, and the conversion-before-traffic rule. The three awareness stages (cold, warm, hot) are older than the source, from Eugene Schwartz's stages of awareness; cross-checked against standard landing-page and offer-design practice. Kulkov's own product figures are his marketing, directional and not citable. The method is what carries.

## Hard rules

- Reads the project's `brand.md` first. Never hardcodes a brand.
- Conversion before traffic, without exception. Never green-light a paid acquisition push before the offer and page convert. That rule is the reason this skill exists.
- Does not decide what to build, and does not set the final price. It surfaces the pricing model that fits retention and the no-brainer framing; the owner sets the number. Anything that spends money is Red.
- The most valuable output is often the unwelcome one: "the leak is the pricing model, not the page," or "this is a positioning problem, no page fixes it." Say it plainly.
- Never edits operating canon (`SOUL.md`, `IDENTITY.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, `SPEC.md`, `memory/`).
- No AI tells, no dashes on any owner-facing copy produced. Run `turma:anti-ai-linguo` as the final pass.
